Week 6: Retrieval and Ranking Architectures

WEEK 06 · IR AND RAG SYSTEMS
From a corpus to a defensible context

This week treats retrieval as a multi-stage systems problem: preserve useful evidence early, then spend expensive computation only where it can change the final answer.

System reasoning

Trace the cascade

Explain the distinct jobs of retrieval, ranking, re-ranking, and context assembly.

Complexity reasoning

Budget the work

Reason about runtime, memory, candidate depth, embedding dimension, and token budget.

Architecture reasoning

Design the hand-offs

Choose what an index, a lightweight ranker, and a deep re-ranker should each know.

Stakeholder reasoning

Audit what is lost

Evaluate recall, provenance, visibility, and source diversity—not only a final relevance score.

Delivery: 2 minutes. State the outcomes as design capabilities rather than a list to memorise. Connect the prior exercise to the first hand-off: current-session signals may be valuable, but they must be available and affordable at the stage where they are used. Preview the notation used today: corpus size N, candidate depths K1, K2, and K3, embedding dimension d, joint sequence length L, and context token budget B.

Why retrieval alone cannot choose the final evidence

Each stage receives fewer items and is allowed to use more expensive evidence. A document removed early cannot be recovered later.

Then assemble: deduplicate, diversify, preserve provenance, and fit evidence within the RAG context budget B.

Delivery: 3 minutes. Retrieval is deliberately broad: it should retain plausible evidence, not make the final semantic distinction. The lightweight ranker makes a first precision-oriented cut with features that are more expensive or less indexable than retrieval features. The deep re-ranker reads query and document together, so it is reserved for a shortlist. Context assembly is not another relevance model: it is a list-level and token-budget decision. Ask: “If a relevant source falls outside K1, can a perfect re-ranker rescue it?” No.

Why not use one deep scoring stage?

Single-stage deep scoring

Apply joint attention to every retrieved candidate

Input: K₁ candidates, each with a query-document sequence of length L.

Approximate attention computeO(K₁L²d)K₁ candidates × quadratic sequence interaction × hidden dimension d

With K₁ = 1,000, every request fetches and jointly processes 1,000 document texts. The most expensive model is spent on many weak candidates.

Staged ranking and re-ranking

Use deep joint scoring only after two cheaper cuts

Retrieve: N → K₁ with an index; exact dense scan is O(Nd), while ANN trades exactness for measured speed.
Rank: K₁ → K₂ with lightweight features, often O(K₁d) or a bounded feature-model cost.
Re-rank: K₂ → K₃ with joint attention, O(K₂L²d).
Deep-scoring reductionK₁ / K₂1,000 → 20 means 50× fewer joint query-document evaluations

Delivery: 3 minutes. Contrast the columns carefully. The left is not merely slow because it has many candidates; self-attention makes each query-document comparison much more expensive than a cached-vector score. The right column does not claim a universal ANN complexity bound: latency and recall depend on index type, hardware, and configuration. The engineering claim is that stages reserve expensive query-document interaction for candidates that survived cheaper evidence. The next sections make these terms concrete with index, embedding, and ranking code.
