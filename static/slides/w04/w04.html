Week 04: Neural Recommendation Models

A neural recommender does not replace the evaluation contract. It replaces a fixed interaction rule with a learned function of the representations and evidence available to it.

Note

Today: compare latent-factor and neural interaction functions, reason about implicit-feedback training, and ask when a model’s score is not yet an explanation.

Delivery: 1 minute. Make the scope clear. The goal is not to memorise every neural architecture; it is to recognise the modelling choice that changes when an inner product becomes a learnable interaction function.

Learning outcomes

Build

Describe a neural recommender as embeddings, an interaction function, and a training objective.

Compare

Contrast a latent-factor dot product with a learned non-linear interaction function.

Interpret

Identify what implicit feedback supports—and what an unobserved interaction cannot prove.

Critique

Assess whether a performance claim, explanation, and safeguard are aligned.

Delivery: 1 minute. Read the action verbs, then use them as signposts through the sections. The final outcome keeps the Week 03 evidence lens active throughout the technical material.

Bridge

01 From Matrix Factorisation to Neural CF

Timing: 0 minutes.

Matrix factorisation: a dot product is an interaction rule

Learn representations

\(\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u}\in\mathbb R^d,\quad\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i}\in\mathbb R^d\)

The axes are learned coordinates—not named traits without evidence.

Choose a fixed interaction

\(\color{navy}{\hat y_{ui}}=\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u}^{\top}\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i}+\color{navy}{b_u+b_i}\)

A dot product rewards aligned coordinates.

Note

If we retain the embeddings but replace the dot product, what can the scorer represent—and what extra evidence or compute will it need?

Timing: 3 minutes. MF already learns embeddings; today’s architectural choice is how to combine them.

[Source] - Koren, Bell, & Volinsky (2009). “Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems.”

Worked MF score: one user, one candidate

Given vectors

\(\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u}=\color{teal}{[0.8,0.2]}\)

\(\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i}=\color{orange}{[0.9,0.1]}\)

Let both biases be zero.

Multiply, then add

\(\color{navy}{\hat y}=\color{teal}{0.8}\color{orange}{(0.9)}+\color{teal}{0.2}\color{orange}{(0.1)}\)

\(=\color{teal}{0.72}+\color{teal}{0.02}=\color{navy}{\mathbf{0.74}}\)

A relative score, not automatically a probability or explanation.

Timing: 4 minutes. Ask students to state the two products before revealing the total.

Three ways to combine the same embeddings

GMF

\(\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u}\odot\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i}\)

Weight coordinate-wise matches.

MLP

\(f(\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u},\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i})\)

Mix all coordinates non-linearly.

NeuMF

\([\mathrm{GMF};\mathrm{MLP}]\)

Combine both paths before prediction.

Timing: 3 minutes. More capacity is a claim to test, not a free gain.

[Source] - He, X. et al. (2017). “Neural Collaborative Filtering.” WWW ’17.

Worked interaction functions: same input, different score

\(\color{teal}{\mathbf p_u}=\color{teal}{[.8,.2]},\quad\color{orange}{\mathbf q_i}=\color{orange}{[.9,.1]}\) — toy weights expose the calculation.

1 · GMF

\(\color{teal}{[.8,.2]}\odot\color{orange}{[.9,.1]}=\color{teal}{[.72,.02]}\)

\(\color{navy}{s_G}=\color{navy}{[1,1]}^\top\color{teal}{[.72,.02]}=\color{navy}{\mathbf{.74}}\)

2 · MLP

\(\color{teal}{\mathbf h}=\operatorname{ReLU}([.8+.1,\.2+.9])=\color{teal}{[.9,1.1]}\)

\(\color{navy}{s_M}=\color{navy}{[.2,.4]}^\top\color{teal}{[.9,1.1]}=\color{navy}{\mathbf{.62}}\)

3 · NeuMF

\(\color{navy}{s_N}=\color{navy}{[.5,.5,.1,.1]}^\top[\color{teal}{.72,.02};\color{teal}{.9,1.1}]\)

\(=\color{teal}{.36+.01+.09+.11}=\color{navy}{\mathbf{.57}}\)

Different scores reflect different toy weights. This demonstrates expressiveness, not a winner.

Timing: 5 minutes. Work left-to-right. GMF multiplies first; MLP combines coordinates; NeuMF combines both representations. Compare only under a shared protocol.

[Source] - He, X. et al. (2017). “Neural Collaborative Filtering.” WWW ’17.

Code lens: the forward pass is the architecture choice

mf_score = (user_embedding[u] * item_embedding[i]).sum()
gmf = user_gmf[u] * item_gmf[i]
mlp = relu(mlp_layers(concat(user_mlp[u], item_mlp[i])))
neumf_score = prediction_layer(concat(gmf, mlp))

Timing: 2 minutes. This is pseudocode: the framework cannot choose the evaluation contract for us.

02 Training for Ranked Recommendations

Timing: 0 minutes.

Implicit feedback creates a sampling decision

Observed positive

Aisha clicked City Gardens.

A signal of possible interest—not a rating of every alternative.

Unobserved candidate

Aisha did not click Riverside Diner.

It may have been unseen, unavailable, badly timed, or unappealing.

logged click→positive+sampled unobserved item→training comparison
Negative sampling is an assumption about missing feedback—not a discovered dislike.

Timing: 4 minutes. Ask for two non-preference explanations of the missing click; sampling changes which comparisons training sees.

[Sources] - Aggarwal (2016), Sections 1.3.1.1 and 3.6.4.6. - Rendle et al. (2009). BPR.

Two training questions, two objectives

Pointwise: is this pair positive?

\(\color{navy}{y_{ui}}\ \text{vs.}\ \color{teal}{\hat y_{ui}}\)

Needs a target for an unobserved pair.

Pairwise: which item ranks higher?

\(\color{teal}{\hat s(u,i^+)}>\color{orange}{\hat s(u,i^-)}\)

Matches a top-k comparison.

Timing: 3 minutes. A scorer such as MF or NeuMF can be trained with either objective.

BPR worked example: make the observed item rank higher

Triplet

\(\color{navy}{u}=\) Aisha; \(\color{teal}{i^+}=\) City Gardens (clicked); \(\color{orange}{i^-}=\) Riverside Diner (sampled).

\(\color{teal}{\hat s(u,i^+)}=1.20,\ \color{orange}{\hat s(u,i^-)}=0.40\)

1 · Margin

\(\color{teal}{\Delta}=\color{teal}{1.20}-\color{orange}{0.40}=\color{navy}{0.80}\)

Positive above sampled alternative.

2 · Pairwise probability

\(\color{teal}{\sigma(\Delta)}=\sigma(\color{navy}{0.80})=\color{teal}{0.690}\)

Model confidence in this ordering.

3 · Loss and update

\(\color{navy}{L_{\mathrm{BPR}}}=-\ln(\color{teal}{0.690})=\color{orange}{0.371}\)

\(\partial L/\partial\Delta=\color{orange}{-0.310}\): gradient descent increases the positive score and/or lowers the sampled score.

BPR ranks the click above this sampled comparison; it does not call the unclicked item “bad.”

Timing: 6 minutes. Work triplet, margin, sigmoid, then negative log. The numbers are pedagogical current-model outputs.

[Source] - Rendle et al. (2009), Eq. 13 and optimisation discussion.

☕ TriRank

TriRank extends the user–item graph with review aspects, modelling users, items, and the aspects mentioned in reviews as a tripartite graph. That extra evidence can support explanations such as “recommended because you value battery life and display quality.”


TriRank was one of the final precursor works to Neural Collaborative Filtering (NeuCF) pioneered here at NUS.

He, Chen, Kan & Chen (2015), “TriRank: Review-aware Explainable Recommendation by Modeling Aspects,” CIKM 2015

05:00

It models user-item-aspect relations extracted from reviews, making it a useful bridge from model structure to explainability. 03 Practical Comparison: Baseline First

Timing: 0 minutes.

The experiment contract

Hold fixed Why Inspect
Data + split Both models face the same evidence. Is test later than training?
Negative sampler Sampling changes the ranking task. Which unseen items become negatives?
Metrics + K Metrics answer stated list-quality questions. Does K match the product surface?
Budget + reporting A gain needs cost and reproducibility context. Time, parameters, seeds, examples.

Warning

Changing the model and the split, sampler, or metric together does not identify what produced an apparent improvement.

Timing: 4 minutes. This is the Week 03 evaluation contract applied to architecture comparison.

04 Capacity Has Costs

Timing: 0 minutes.

When is neural complexity justified?

Question Supports capacity Retain simpler baseline
Signal Held-out gain persists under declared protocol. Gain is small, unstable, or split-specific.
Operations Latency, memory, retraining fit budget. Gain costs too much complexity.
Accountability Team can document data, failures, safeguards. No honest explanation or audit.

Timing: 4 minutes. A neural model is not a default upgrade.

Ethics debrief: a score is not an explanation

Overclaim

“This item is recommended because the model understands your preferences.”

Accountable claim

“Under stated data, objective, and ranking policy, the model assigned this item a high score.”

Warning

Capacity makes a scorer more flexible; it does not make logged interactions complete, causal, or value-neutral evidence about a person.

Timing: 4 minutes. Distinguish faithful inputs from a causal explanation of preference.

Summary

🔑 Architecture and objective are separate choices.

1 · Represent
Embeddings provide inputs.
2 · Interact
MF dot product or neural function.
3 · Optimise
BPR trains a relative ranking claim.
4 · Justify
Keep costs and limits visible.
