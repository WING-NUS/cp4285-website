For your selected recommendation surface, critically analyse and propose a technically credible recommender design for the particular recommendation aspect you have selected to focus on. Draw on the Week 01–02 material (Collaborative Filtering & Latent Factor Models) in constructing and defending your argument.

The structure and emphasis of the report are deliberately yours to determine. Your task is to make a focused technical argument, rather than to follow a fixed template. You should decide what evidence, explanation, comparison, and level of implementation detail are necessary to persuade an informed reader that the proposed approach is appropriate for the case.

You should not describe a **complete** production recommender system; rather, select one to two technical concepts most consequential to your chosen surface and develop a precise, evidence-based argument.

The case study should be grounded in the selected platform’s actual product setting, but it is not an exercise in recovering a proprietary algorithm. Clearly distinguish **documented facts** (if any) about the platform from your own **reasoned technical proposal**. The work should engage seriously with algorithmic choice, the realities of operating a recommender over a large and changing catalogue _I_, and the relationship between technical choices and inferred recommendation objectives.

You must also return to the **Week 01 slides** and select one of the card-suit archetypes to use as a substantive analytical lens. The four archetypes are deliberately not repeated here. The selected lens must feature in your technical design choice, objective, constraint, feature, ranking rule, or evaluation criterion; merely defining the archetype is insufficient.

> **Independent-learning extension.** Your critique should also describe the design's connection to operating **at scale** and remaining **responsive** to fresh user behaviour, changing items, and relevant context, as **secondary** design considerations. Note that our course has **not** taught production recommender-system serving, latency engineering, or responsiveness in detail (you are welcome to use the optional readings to help with this).

### AI Use, Ownership, and Verification

**AI use is encouraged.** Use it as a research and thinking partner to identify vocabulary, locate possible lines of inquiry, explain unfamiliar production concepts, generate alternative technical designs, test counterarguments, and improve the clarity of your draft. It is not evidence, however, and all AI-generated claims and citations must be verified against the original sources.

You must **lead the investigation**. Before using AI substantively, establish the scaffolding for its work: identify the recommendation surface, set a provisional question or claim, state the technical issue you wish to investigate, and give the system a focused task. Your prompts should steer the inquiry rather than ask AI to produce an unexamined essay. The central idea, interpretation of the case, technical judgment, organisation, and final argument must be your own. AI may inform your thinking, but it must not replace the ideation that gives the essay its intellectual direction.

Attach an **AI-use declaration** after the reference list. It must identify the tool(s) used; explain how you framed, constrained, or scaffolded the AI’s investigation; state how AI contributed; and give two examples of outputs that you checked, corrected, or rejected. Retain your relevant prompts and outputs in case they are requested.

Students may be asked to discuss or explain their essay, its sources, its technical choices, and their use of AI. This is an opportunity to demonstrate ownership of the work. The instructor’s academic judgment, including the determination of authorship, the assessment of the work, and the final grade, is final.
